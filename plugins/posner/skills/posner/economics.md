# Law and economics: the ideas behind the opinions

This file summarizes, in our own words, the main economic and jurisprudential ideas that Judge Posner developed in his books and articles. It contains no passages copied from those works. Use it to supply the economic reasoning an analysis needs, and attribute an idea to the work it comes from (for example, "Posner argued in *Economic Analysis of Law* that..."). Do not quote these works, and do not present an idea here as something he held on a question he never addressed.

Citations are to first editions unless noted. Check the edition and page before relying on a citation in anything filed or published.

## 1. Efficiency as a guide to law (EAL; Economics of Justice)

- **Wealth maximization.** Posner proposed that many common law rules can be explained, and judged, by whether they move resources to the uses people value most, measured by willingness to pay. He offered this as both a description of what common law judges had tended to do and, for a time, as a normative standard. He later softened the normative claim and folded it into his pragmatism (Problems of Jurisprudence; Overcoming Law).
- **The common law tends toward efficiency.** His positive thesis was that judge-made doctrines in property, contract and tort often look like what a planner seeking efficiency would choose, partly because inefficient rules generate more litigation and so more chances to be revised.
- **Rules as prices.** A liability rule sets an expected cost on conduct. The question is what behavior that price will produce in future parties, not only what is fair between the two in court.
- **Transaction costs decide the remedy.** Following Coase, he stressed that when parties can bargain cheaply, the initial assignment of a right matters less, because they will trade to the efficient result. When bargaining is costly, the law should assign the right to the party who would have bought it, or set a liability rule that mimics the bargain.

## 2. Torts (Theory of Negligence; Tort Law with Landes)

- **Negligence as cost-benefit.** He read the Learned Hand formula as the core of negligence: liability when the burden of a precaution is less than the expected loss it would prevent. The rule gives potential injurers an incentive to take cost-justified care and no more.
- **Care versus activity level.** Negligence controls how carefully people act, but not how much or where they engage in a risky activity. Strict liability is the better tool when accidents are best reduced by doing less of an activity or moving it, which courts cannot easily judge under a negligence test (see Indiana Harbor Belt in the library).
- **Contributory fault and the cheaper avoider.** Rules that share or shift liability should push the loss onto the party who could have avoided it most cheaply.

## 3. Contracts and remedies (EAL)

- **Contract as risk allocation.** A contract allocates the risk of future events. Where the contract is silent, a court should supply the term the parties would likely have chosen, usually by placing the risk on the party who could prevent or insure against it more cheaply.
- **Efficient breach.** Expectation damages let a promisor break a contract when performance has become worth less than the promisee's loss, pay that loss, and leave both no worse off than performance. Overcompensatory remedies deter breaches that would make society better off.
- **Penalty clauses.** He doubted that the penalty doctrine serves a good economic purpose, since sophisticated parties may have reasons for a large stipulated sum, but he applied the doctrine where state law required it (Lake River in the library).
- **Specific performance and injunctions.** An injunction makes sense where damages are hard to measure, but it can create bargaining leverage that exceeds the value of the right, a point he applied to patent remedies (Apple v. Motorola in the library).

## 4. Antitrust (Antitrust Law; Chicago School; Social Costs of Monopoly)

- **Consumer welfare and efficiency.** He argued antitrust should aim at economic efficiency, chiefly preventing output restriction and price increases, not at protecting competitors or small firms as such.
- **Skepticism of older theories.** He questioned theories that treated vertical restraints, tying and price discrimination as presumptively harmful, arguing that many of these practices have efficiency explanations. He later took a more mixed view than some Chicago School writers, for example on the dangers of collusion and on the costs of monopoly.
- **The social cost of monopoly.** Beyond the classic deadweight loss, he argued that firms spend resources to obtain and keep monopoly power, and those expenditures can dissipate much of the monopoly profit, so the total social cost may be larger than standard estimates.
- **Collusion as the central concern.** He favored aggressive action against price fixing and treated tacit coordination in concentrated markets as a real problem (see High Fructose Corn Syrup in the library).
- **Error costs.** Because courts can mistake competitive conduct for exclusion, antitrust rules should weigh the cost of false positives, which deter aggressive competition, against false negatives.

## 5. Intellectual property (IP Law with Landes; Copyright with Landes)

- **The basic trade-off.** IP rights encourage creation by letting creators recover their costs, but they raise prices and the cost of building on earlier work. The optimal right is the one that balances incentives to create against access costs, including the costs to later creators.
- **Costs of expression and tracing.** Landes and Posner analyzed copyright doctrines (idea and expression, fair use, duration) as devices for keeping those access and administrative costs down.
- **Trademarks.** They treated trademarks as tools that lower consumer search costs, which explains why protection follows consumer confusion rather than ownership of a word.
- **Patents and excess.** They argued that patent protection may be stronger than needed in industries where innovation would occur anyway, and that broad rights can encourage wasteful races and defensive patenting. Posner raised this view publicly while sitting by designation in patent cases.
- **Remedies.** The clearest statement of his view that a patent remedy should not let the owner capture more than the invention's value is his opinion in Apple v. Motorola (library), not a passage in these books. Cite the opinion for that point. The general framework, that an injunction can create bargaining leverage beyond the value of the right, comes from EAL (section 3 above).

## 6. Pragmatism and judging (Problems of Jurisprudence; Overcoming Law; Pragmatism and Democracy; How Judges Think)

- **Legal pragmatism.** He described good judging as forward-looking and practical: choose the decision with the best consequences, taking full account of systemic consequences such as the value of legal stability and predictability. Precedent and text are important inputs, not mechanical answers.
- **Skepticism of legal formalism.** He doubted that legal reasoning alone, through deduction from text or precedent, can decide hard cases. When the materials run out, judges legislate in the gaps whether they admit it or not, and they should do so openly and sensibly.
- **Skepticism of moral theory.** He argued that abstract moral philosophy gives judges little help in deciding cases and that practical, empirical inquiry serves better.
- **How judges actually decide.** In *How Judges Think* he described judges as constrained but not fully bound by law, influenced by temperament, experience, ideology and institutional incentives, and argued that candor about this would improve judging.
- **Empirical humility.** He urged judges to learn the facts about the world their rulings affect, including technology and markets, and to use social science where it is reliable.

## How the skill should use this file

1. Use section 1 for the general move from fairness between the parties to incentives for future parties.
2. Use the section for the subject at hand (torts, contracts, antitrust, IP) to frame the economics.
3. Use section 6 when the question is how a court should handle a gap, a foreign framework, or a formalist rule.
4. Attribute each idea to its work by short name or full title. Paraphrase only.
5. Ideas here are his scholarship, not holdings. Pair them with an opinion from the library when one is on point, and say so when none is.
