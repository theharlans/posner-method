# Worked example

This is an unedited run of test case 1 (see `tests/cases.md`), produced on November 17, 2026.

**Input**

/posner A company owns a patent it declared essential to a cellular standard and gave a Fair, Reasonable and Non-Discriminatory ("FRAND") licensing commitment to the European Telecommunications Standards Institute ("ETSI"). It offered a license to a phone maker, which rejected the offer as too high and kept selling phones. The patent holder now asks a US court for an injunction. Should the court grant it?

**Output**

**Issue.** A patent holder promised the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI) that it would license its standard-essential patent (SEP) to anyone on Fair, Reasonable and Non-Discriminatory (FRAND) terms. A phone maker turned down its offer as too high and kept selling phones. The question is whether a US court should order those sales stopped, or whether money is enough to compensate the patent holder.

**The economics.** The patent holder and the phone maker agree that a license should exist. They disagree only about the price. An injunction does nothing to answer that question. What it does is give the patent holder a weapon in the negotiation. Here is a rough illustration, and the numbers are guesses. Suppose the patented feature is worth $0.25 a phone measured before the standard was adopted. The phone sells for $500 and earns the maker $50. Once the industry has built to the standard, the maker cannot design around the patent. So the threat of halting sales lets the holder ask for something closer to the $50 margin than to the $0.25 value of its technology. That gap is hold-up value. It comes from the standard, not from the invention, and the FRAND promise was made to keep it out of the price.

The patent holder is also the cheaper cost avoider. It decided to make the FRAND commitment, and in exchange its patent was written into a standard used across the world. That deal left it with the risk that a licensee would dispute the rate and that a court would end up setting it. Error costs point the same way. If an injunction is wrongly granted, a product line comes off the market and the holder collects hold-up money. If an injunction is wrongly denied, the holder gets a court-set royalty with interest, possibly some time later. The first error costs far more than the second.

There is a cost on the other side, and it is real. If injunctions are never available, phone makers have a reason to hold out: refuse every offer, litigate, and pay at worst the rate they would have paid anyway, years late. The answer to that problem is a damages award that makes holding out expensive, through prejudgment interest and enhancement for bad faith. Withholding the product from consumers is not needed to solve it.

**Pragmatic outcome.** The better rule treats a FRAND commitment as strong evidence that a royalty is adequate compensation. The patent holder's remedy is a court-set FRAND royalty for past sales and an ongoing royalty for future sales. An injunction stays available, but only against a phone maker that will not pay a rate the court has set, or that cannot be made to pay it. Under this rule the patent holder bears the costs of delay and of litigating the rate. Consumers keep their phones, and future SEP holders cannot price their patents at the value of the standard.

**Likely holding.** Injunction denied. Because the patent holder made a FRAND commitment, it cannot show irreparable harm or that money damages are inadequate. A reasonable royalty fully compensates it. Rejecting an offer as too high is not refusing a license. It is the ordinary dispute over price that the FRAND framework expects courts to resolve. If the request is for a preliminary injunction, the result is the same and the reasoning is quicker. Hp, the harm to the plaintiff without an injunction, is small and can be compensated with money. Hd, the harm to the defendant from a wrongly granted injunction, is large, because it means halted sales and hold-up leverage. So P x Hp falls short of (1 - P) x Hd even if P, the plaintiff's chance of winning, is high.

**The formalist objection.** The Patent Act gives the patentee a right to exclude others. The ETSI policy, read as a contract, nowhere says the holder gives up injunctions. And the equitable test for patent injunctions is decided case by case, without categorical rules. A court that refuses SEP injunctions as a class is writing a waiver into the contract that the parties never put there. Most of this should be conceded. There is no categorical bar, and nothing in the policy expressly waives injunctions. The reply is that the analysis above does not depend on a waiver. It runs through the ordinary equitable factors. A holder that has promised to license everyone at a reasonable royalty has already said that money is what it wants. That makes irreparable harm hard to prove on the facts, whatever the text of the policy says. The objection does win in one setting: a phone maker that refuses to pay even a court-set FRAND rate.

**From the opinions.**
- Apple, Inc. v. Motorola, Inc., 869 F. Supp. 2d 901 (N.D. Ill. 2012): a FRAND commitment concedes that a royalty is adequate compensation, so no injunction, and the royalty should exclude hold-up value.
- American Hospital Supply Corp. v. Hospital Products Ltd., 780 F.2d 589 (7th Cir. 1986): interim relief turns on the formula P x Hp > (1 - P) x Hd, which weighs the cost of a wrongly granted injunction against the cost of a wrongly denied one.
- Northern Indiana Public Service Co. v. Carbon County Coal Co., 799 F.2d 265 (7th Cir. 1986): a party that accepted a risk as part of a bargain keeps it when the bet goes against it. Here the bargain is the FRAND commitment in exchange for inclusion in the standard.

**What would change the answer.**
1. The phone maker refuses to pay, or to be bound by, whatever FRAND rate the court sets. That is a real refusal to license, not a dispute over price.
2. A money judgment cannot be collected, because the phone maker is insolvent or beyond the court's reach.
3. The offer was plainly within the FRAND range and the phone maker made no counteroffer and dragged out negotiations in bad faith. That would point to holdout rather than hold-up.
