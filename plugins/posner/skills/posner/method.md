# The method

This is an independent summary, in our own words, of the analytical habits visible across Judge Posner's published opinions. It quotes none of his books or articles.

## 1. Find the real stakes

- Restate the facts without doctrinal labels. What happened, who lost what, and what each side wants the court to do.
- Put a number on it where you can: the dollars at issue, the cost of a precaution, the price difference between two offers.
- Separate the issue the parties argue about from the issue that decides the case.

## 2. Look forward, not back

- A legal rule is a price on future conduct. Ask what each candidate rule will cause the next hundred parties in this position to do.
- Ask who will change behavior in response: the injurer, the victim, the licensor, the licensee, the insurer.
- Prefer the rule that produces sensible behavior ex ante, even if it seems harsh to one party ex post.

## 3. Weigh costs and benefits

- List the costs and benefits of each candidate rule, including the costs of administering it (litigation, proof, uncertainty) and the cost of courts getting it wrong.
- Two kinds of error matter: wrongly imposing liability and wrongly withholding it. Ask which is more costly here, and how often each is likely.
- Rough estimates are better than none. Say when a number is a guess.

## 4. Find the cheaper cost avoider

- Ask which party could have prevented the loss, or insured against it, at lower cost.
- Contracts: a fixed-price or fixed-quantity term allocates risk. The party that took the risk usually keeps it, even when the bet goes badly.
- Torts: negligence asks whether the defendant skipped a precaution worth taking; strict liability makes sense when the injurer can best reduce accidents by changing the level or location of the activity, not just by taking more care.

## 5. Use a formal tool where it fits

- Negligence (the Learned Hand formula): a party is negligent if B < P x L, where B is the burden of the untaken precaution, P the probability of the harm, and L the size of the loss.
- Preliminary relief: grant the injunction only if P x Hp > (1 - P) x Hd, where P is the probability the plaintiff wins, Hp the irreparable harm to the plaintiff without the injunction, and Hd the irreparable harm to the defendant from a wrongly granted injunction. (See American Hospital Supply in the library.)
- Damages: expected value. A claimed lost profit is worth the probability it would have been earned times its amount, and a court may reject it entirely when the probability cannot be estimated with reasonable confidence.
- Do not force a formula onto a question it does not fit. Say why it does not fit.

## 6. Treat precedent as a constraint with a purpose

- Identify the holding (what was necessary to decide the case) and set aside dicta.
- Ask what problem the rule was meant to solve. Apply it in a way that serves that purpose.
- If binding precedent or a statute compels a result the economics would not, say so plainly, apply the law, and note the cost. (Lake River in the library shows this move: skepticism of the penalty doctrine, then application of it because state law required it.)

## 7. Choose the best consequences the law permits

- The bottom line is the outcome with the best overall consequences that is consistent with the governing law, fairly read.
- Name who bears the cost of the chosen rule.

## 8. Meet the formalist objection head on

- State the strongest argument from text, structure, canons, or precedent in its best form, as its proponent would.
- Then answer it on the merits, or concede it and explain what follows.
- Do not attack a weak version of the other side.

## 9. Test the evidence on its method

- For an expert, ask: what question did the model answer, what data did it use, what did it assume, and would the result survive a change in a key assumption?
- A credentialed expert with an unreliable method gets no deference. A judge has a duty to screen the method, not just the conclusion.
- Statistical results need a sound comparison: the right baseline, the right controls, and a sample that fits the question.

## 10. Write plainly

- Short sentences. Concrete examples. Everyday words.
- No Latin where English works. Define any term of art in a clause.
- Be candid about uncertainty and about the weak points in your own analysis.
- Lead each section with its point.
