**Issue.** Licensing companies sued three wireless carriers for about $436 million on three patents they said were essential to the 4G and 5G standards. They lost on infringement and, in part, on validity, and the jury also found they breached their ETSI FRAND undertaking. On appeal they ask the Seventh Circuit to set aside the breach finding as unnecessary, as a matter for the judge, and as wrong because their rate was reasonable. The question underneath is whether a patent holder that loses on its patents can still be held to account for how it tried to license them.

**The economics.** Start with the premise. A FRAND undertaking is a promise about terms and conditions, not only a rate. ETSI's policy (clause 6.1) speaks of licenses on "fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory ("FRAND") terms and conditions." The rate is one of those terms, often the most contested, but only one.

A legal rule is a price on future conduct. The holders propose a safe harbor: any rate within a reasonable range cannot breach FRAND. That rule prices only the visible term, so it invites holders to move value out of the rate and into everything else. A holder could offer a defensible headline number and recover the difference through a broad grant-back, a portfolio bundle that sweeps in nonessential or expired patents, worldwide scope where the implementer needs one country, punitive back royalties for past use, or a nondisclosure term that keeps the implementer from learning what comparable licensees pay. Any of these can move as much money as a higher rate. Discrimination can also be in process. Two licensees can face the same headline rate while one gets information, time, and a real counteroffer, and the other gets an ultimatum.

The first argument has a similar incentive problem. If a breach finding falls away whenever the holder loses on infringement, then a holder's exposure for a non-FRAND offer depends on how strong its patents are, not on how it behaved. The weaker the patents, the safer the bad offer. That is backward. The finding also has value beyond this case: the carriers and their equipment suppliers will likely meet these holders again on other patents, and a judgment about the past offers shapes the next negotiation. That value, though, depends on whether the finding carries preclusive effect, which turns on whether it was necessary to a judgment.

The holders are the cheaper cost avoider. They write the offer, they know their comparable licenses, and they can explain both at low cost. Implementer holdout is a real risk on the other side, but the answer is a good faith duty that runs both ways, which a jury can apply to implementers too.

Error costs sort the judge-jury question. A jury asked to pick a royalty from competing economic models can err badly, and that error is expensive. A jury asked whether a party explained its offer, treated similar licensees alike, or insisted on terms no comparable licensee accepted is doing ordinary breach of contract fact finding, which juries do every day.

**Pragmatic outcome.** The better rule keeps a FRAND breach finding when it answers a claim of its own, divides the labor so that the judge states what the undertaking requires and the jury decides whether the holders' conduct met it, and refuses any safe harbor based on the rate alone. Holders bear the risk of being judged on the whole offer and the whole negotiation. Implementers bear the same duty of good faith.

**Likely holding.** Affirmed, on these grounds.

1. *Necessity.* If the carriers or the intervening suppliers pleaded breach as a counterclaim, it is a separate claim with its own judgment, and non-infringement does not moot it. The FRAND obligation attaches to the declared patents and the holders' licensing conduct, not to whether these particular products infringe. The holders asserted essentiality in order to sue; they cannot then disclaim it to escape the undertaking that came with it. If breach was raised only as a defense to the infringement claim, the court would affirm on non-infringement and invalidity and need not review the breach finding at all. A finding unnecessary to the judgment has little preclusive force, so vacating it would accomplish nothing.
2. *Judge or jury.* Largely rejected. What French law requires of an ETSI undertaking is a question of law for the judge (Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 44.1 treats the content of foreign law that way), delivered through jury instructions. Whether these offers and this conduct met that standard is a question of fact for the jury in an action for breach. Setting the terms of a license is a different task, and it was not before this jury.
3. *Rate within range.* Rejected. A reasonable rate is necessary but not sufficient. A jury could find breach through non-price terms, discrimination in treatment, or bad faith in negotiation, even with a defensible rate.

The judgment stands unless the instructions misstated French law or let the jury find breach on the rate alone without a standard. In that event the remedy is a new trial on the breach claim only.

**The formalist objection.** In its strongest form: the ETSI undertaking is a contract between the holder and ETSI, and the carriers are strangers to it. FRAND states no price and no method for finding one. A promise that leaves its essential terms open is too indefinite to enforce, and asking lay jurors whether an offer was "fair" is rate-setting in disguise. The court should decide the patent questions and stop.

The reply has three parts. First, clause 12 makes French law govern the policy, and a federal court must apply that law whatever its own view of the economics. Under the French Civil Code as reformed in 2016, a promise may be made for the benefit of a third party (art. 1205), the beneficiary has a direct right against the promisor (art. 1206), and contracts must be negotiated, formed and performed in good faith (art. 1104). Second, the undertaking does not promise a price. It promises a kind of offer and a way of negotiating, and courts enforce standards of conduct (good faith, reasonable efforts) without fixing a price all the time. Third, the holders' indefiniteness argument cuts against their own third argument: if FRAND were only a rate, it would be more indefinite, not less.

Two concessions. If the jury was told only "FRAND" and asked whether the offer was fair, with no instruction on what terms and conduct French law reaches, the objection wins and the claim must be retried. And economics runs out on what "fair" means between a licensing company and a carrier with different business models, or how to weigh fair process against an efficient price. There the decision rests on contract text, good faith, and institutional fit, not on a model.

**From the opinions.**
- Apple, Inc. v. Motorola, Inc., 869 F. Supp. 2d 901 (N.D. Ill. 2012): a FRAND commitment concedes that a royalty is adequate compensation and the royalty must exclude hold-up value, which shows the commitment has legal force, though the opinion addressed remedies, not breach.
- Lake River Corp. v. Carborundum Co., 769 F.2d 1284 (7th Cir. 1985): the court applies the governing law even when the economics points elsewhere; here clause 12 makes French law decide what the undertaking means.
- ATA Airlines, Inc. v. Federal Express Corp., 665 F.3d 882 (7th Cir. 2011): a jury verdict on an agreement lacking an essential price term was reversed, the best support for the formalist objection, distinguished because a FRAND undertaking promises a standard of conduct rather than an agreed price.
- No opinion in the library is directly on point on whether a FRAND breach finding survives non-infringement, or on how judge and jury divide a FRAND claim.
- In *Economic Analysis of Law*, Posner treated a contract as an allocation of risk and argued that a court filling a gap should place the risk on the party who can avoid or insure against it more cheaply, here the holder that drafts the offer.

**What would change the answer.**
1. The breach issue was raised only as a defense, not as a counterclaim. The court would then affirm on the patent grounds and leave the breach finding unreviewed and without preclusive effect.
2. The instructions treated FRAND as a rate alone, or misstated French law on good faith or third-party enforcement. That calls for a new trial on breach.
3. The record shows the only complaint was the rate, the rate sat within the range shown by comparable licenses, and the non-price terms and the negotiation were unremarkable. The verdict would then lack evidentiary support. The reverse would strengthen it: for example, evidence that the holders refused to offer licenses to the intervening suppliers, which would raise a scope question under ETSI's undertaking to license at least to make, sell, repair, use and operate.
