**Issue.** A holder of patents it says are essential to the High Efficiency Video Coding ("HEVC") standard, bound by a reasonable and non-discriminatory ("RAND") commitment under the Common Patent Policy shared by the International Telecommunication Union ("ITU"), the International Organization for Standardization ("ISO") and the International Electrotechnical Commission ("IEC"), sued without first identifying the patent and explaining the infringement. Should that omission bar an injunction in a US court, when the parties had negotiated over the portfolio for years and the holder asks only for an injunction that bites if the defendant refuses a RAND license?

A threshold point: patent infringement appeals go to the Federal Circuit, so the Seventh Circuit would see this only by hypothesis or through a contract claim on the commitment. The analysis is the same.

The analysis starts from a premise. RAND, like Fair, Reasonable and Non-Discriminatory ("FRAND"), is a promise about the terms and conditions of a license. The Common Patent Policy speaks of negotiating "on a non-discriminatory basis on reasonable terms and conditions." The rate is one term. Pre-suit notice sits on the process side of that promise. It is part of dealing in good faith and of treating every implementer the same way in offers, information and timing, not a step in haggling over price.

**The economics.** *What notice buys.* The holder knows which patents it owns, which claims it relies on, and how it reads them onto the products. It is the cheaper source of that information. A notice letter with a claim chart costs little, perhaps tens of thousands of dollars in lawyer time (a guess). It lets the implementer assess the patent, design around it, challenge validity, or take a license before it faces an exclusion threat. Looking forward, a notice rule costs future holders almost nothing and screens out ambush suits.

*What a rigid notice rule costs.* If the defendant already knew which patent was asserted and how it read on its products, a formal letter adds nothing. Denying an injunction for its absence is then a penalty with no harm behind it, and it gives the defendant a reason to hold out. The sensible price of a missed letter is the information it would have supplied.

*But portfolio talks are not automatically notice.* Portfolio negotiations often run on patent lists, counts and a few sample claim charts. Knowing that a large portfolio is on the table is not the same as knowing that this patent reads on these laptops and phones. Whether the defendant had that knowledge is a question of fact. The holder is the cheaper party to prove it, because it has the record of what it sent. The burden should sit there.

*The conditional injunction.* Hold-up comes from a threat to exclude before the terms are fixed. Rough numbers, all guesses: the defendant ships 30 million HEVC devices a year, and the parties are $0.10 a unit apart, so $3 million a year is in dispute. The devices earn far more, so an unconditional threat to stop sales lets the holder press well beyond that gap. An injunction that takes effect only if the defendant refuses a license on terms a neutral has found RAND removes most of that leverage, because the defendant can always avoid exclusion by accepting reasonable terms. What is left is a stick against holdout, which a damages-only remedy handles poorly when a defendant would rather pay later.

So the conditional request largely cures hold-up, on two conditions. First, the trigger must be refusal of terms the court (or an agreed arbitrator) has set, not refusal of the holder's own offer. If "refusing a RAND license" means refusing what the holder demanded, the leverage is back. Second, the court-set license must cover the material terms, not only the rate. A court-set rate that leaves scope, geography, term, grant-back and confidentiality open lets the holder rebuild hold-up through the non-price terms. Courts can set a rate and core scope from comparable licenses; the rest can go to the parties under a policed duty of good faith, or to arbitration.

The conditional form also moots most of the notice concern. Litigation delivers the notice. By the time the condition could trigger, the defendant has seen the patent, the infringement finding and the terms, so no ambush is possible.

*Error costs.* An injunction wrongly granted pulls products and hands the holder hold-up value. One wrongly denied leaves damages and interest. The first error costs more, and a decree tied to court-set terms makes it unlikely.

*Where the economics runs out.* Whether skipping a step disrespects the process the commitment promises is not a pricing question. There the analysis rests on the policy text and good faith.

**Pragmatic outcome.** Lack of pre-suit notice should weigh in equity in proportion to the information it would have added, not act as an automatic bar. If the portfolio talks identified this patent and explained the infringement, notice served its purpose. If not, the omission points toward damages alone. The holder bears the cost of proof and delay. The defendant faces exclusion only if it refuses terms a neutral found reasonable.

*The European result.* The Unified Patent Court's ("UPC's") Milan Local Division applied the Court of Justice of the European Union's ("CJEU's") Huawei v. ZTE framework, which construes the abuse-of-dominance rule in Article 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union ("TFEU"). There notice is the first step of a fixed sequence, and on the facts given the court treated the missing notice as decisive. The UPC was bound to apply that framework. The result is less harsh than it looks. The holder kept a declaration of infringement, an entitlement to damages and an accounting. It lost only exclusion, which is close to where US equity lands for a RAND holder anyway.

The difference is the route. A US court would borrow the information-forcing idea, treat notice as evidence of good faith, and use negotiating conduct to sort hold-up from holdout. It would not borrow the competition-law foundation, the rigid sequence, or forfeiture without asking what the defendant knew. The CJEU ruling is not binding, and since both systems deny or condition exclusion, conflicting decrees are unlikely.

**Likely holding.** The absence of a pre-suit notice letter does not, by itself, bar equitable relief. A RAND commitment is strong evidence that a royalty is adequate compensation, so an injunction is ordinarily unavailable. The holder's negotiating conduct, including whether it identified the asserted patent and explained the infringement before suing, bears on the equities. Where the holder shows that the defendant knew the patent and the infringement theory from prior negotiations, the lack of a formal letter weighs little. The district court may enter an injunction that takes effect only if the defendant declines, within a set period, a license on terms the court has determined to be RAND. An injunction conditioned on the holder's own offer is not permitted.

**The formalist objection.** In its best form: neither the Patent Act nor the Common Patent Policy says anything about pre-suit notice. The policy commits the holder to be willing to negotiate on reasonable terms and conditions, nothing more. Where Congress wanted notice in patent law, it said so, for damages on unmarked products (35 U.S.C. § 287(a)), and it made the filing of suit count as notice. A court that demands a pre-suit letter writes a term into the contract and the statute.

Most of this is conceded, and the holding imposes no notice gate. But equity looks at conduct, and a promise to negotiate on reasonable terms fairly includes giving the other side what it needs to evaluate the claim. So notice counts as evidence. The opposite objection, that a Huawei-style bright line is cheaper than a fight over years of confidential talks, is partly right. Placing the burden on the holder that skipped the letter keeps most of that incentive.

**From the opinions.**
- Apple, Inc. v. Motorola, Inc., 869 F. Supp. 2d 901 (N.D. Ill. 2012): a FRAND commitment concedes that a royalty is adequate compensation, which cuts against an injunction and keeps hold-up value out of the remedy.
- American Hospital Supply Corp. v. Hospital Products Ltd., 780 F.2d 589 (7th Cir. 1986): equitable relief weighs the cost of a wrongly granted injunction against a wrongly denied one, each by its likelihood.
- Lake River Corp. v. Carborundum Co., 769 F.2d 1284 (7th Cir. 1985): separate what the economics suggests from what binding law requires, which explains why the UPC applied Huawei and a US court need not.
- In *Economic Analysis of Law*, Posner argued that an injunction can create bargaining leverage beyond the value of the underlying right.

**What would change the answer.**
1. The portfolio talks never identified this patent or mapped it to the products. The omission then weighs heavily against an injunction.
2. The decree turns on the holder's own offer or on a rate alone. Hold-up then returns.
3. The defendant refuses to be bound by court-set RAND terms, or a judgment cannot be collected. An injunction is then justified whatever the notice history.
