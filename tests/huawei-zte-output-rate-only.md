**Issue.** A standard essential patent ("SEP") holder that promised to license on Fair, Reasonable and Non-Discriminatory ("FRAND") terms seeks an injunction, and a party asks the court to make the Court of Justice of the European Union ("CJEU") sequence in *Huawei v. ZTE* the test for when one may issue. The question is whether a US court should adopt a foreign competition-law checklist as its injunction rule, or use it some other way. (A candid preliminary point: appeals in cases with patent infringement claims go to the Federal Circuit, so the Seventh Circuit would most likely meet this question in a breach-of-FRAND contract or antitrust suit. The analysis takes the hypothetical as posed.)

**The economics.** The steps aim at a real problem with two sides. Hold-up: the threat of an injunction lets the holder charge for the value of the standard rather than the value of its invention. Holdout: if injunctions are never available, the implementer can stall, litigate, and pay at worst the same rate years late. Huawei's best feature is that it sorts the two by conduct and puts each duty on the party that can perform it most cheaply. The holder knows its portfolio and its other licenses, so it must state a royalty and show the math. The implementer knows its own sales, so it must account for past use and post security. That is a sensible allocation of information costs.

The weakness is the sanction. An implementer that misses a step loses its protection and faces exclusion from the market. An injunction is a poor penalty for slow negotiating, because its leverage tracks the implementer's margin on the product, not the value of the patent. A rough illustration, with guessed numbers: a portfolio worth $10 million a year in FRAND royalties, and an implementer that stalls three years. The holder's loss from delay is the time value of $30 million, a few million dollars at ordinary interest rates, which prejudgment interest and enhancement for bad faith can cover. An injunction against a product line earning hundreds of millions threatens a loss many times that size. The steps are also costly to administer. Each one ("specific," "diligently," "good faith," "prompt") is a standard a court must apply, and whether an offer was FRAND is the very question in dispute. Error costs are lopsided. A wrongly granted injunction pulls products and transfers hold-up value. A wrongly denied one delays a royalty that money can make whole.

**Pragmatic outcome.** Use the steps as evidence, not as the test. Who made a concrete offer, who countered, and who refused to post security are the best available proof of whether the implementer is a willing licensee. But completing the steps should not entitle a holder to an injunction against an implementer that will pay a court-set rate. The most useful part of Huawei, security plus an accounting, can be ordered directly as the price of withholding an injunction. Holders bear the cost of delay and of litigating the rate, consumers keep the products, and holdouts pay interest and risk enhancement.

**Likely holding.** The Huawei steps are not adopted as the governing test. Huawei decides when seeking an injunction is an abuse of a dominant position under Article 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union ("TFEU"). It does not decide when a US court sitting in equity should grant one. A FRAND commitment is strong evidence that a royalty is adequate compensation. Conduct of the kind the Huawei steps describe is relevant to whether the implementer is an unwilling licensee, and an injunction may issue against one that refuses to pay, or to secure, a court-determined FRAND royalty. For preliminary relief, P x Hp > (1 - P) x Hd will rarely be met: Hp is compensable delay, while Hd is a halted product line.

**The formalist objection.** The strongest version rests on uniformity and predictability. The European Telecommunications Standards Institute ("ETSI") commitment is one promise made worldwide, global portfolios are licensed in one deal, and the CJEU's steps are the most developed account of what that promise requires in practice. A US court that goes its own way invites forum shopping and conflicting obligations, and a clear sequence is cheaper to follow than case-by-case equity. Part of this should be conceded: predictability has value, and a court can say that a party that followed the steps will rarely be found to have acted in bad faith. The reply is that Huawei answers a different question in a different legal system. It asks when a dominant firm's litigation is abusive, and it treats exclusion as the default once the implementer falters. US equity starts from whether money damages are adequate, and the Patent Act's right to exclude cuts against any categorical rule, Huawei's included. One concession is unconditional. If the law governing the commitment (the ETSI policy is governed by French law) made the steps contract terms, a court would enforce them and note the cost.

**From the opinions.**
- Apple, Inc. v. Motorola, Inc., 869 F. Supp. 2d 901 (N.D. Ill. 2012): a FRAND commitment concedes that a royalty is adequate compensation, so no injunction, and the royalty excludes hold-up value.
- American Hospital Supply Corp. v. Hospital Products Ltd., 780 F.2d 589 (7th Cir. 1986): interim relief turns on P x Hp > (1 - P) x Hd, weighing a wrongly granted injunction against a wrongly denied one.
- Lake River Corp. v. Carborundum Co., 769 F.2d 1284 (7th Cir. 1985): doubt a rule's economics, but apply it where governing law requires, as would follow if contract law built the steps into the FRAND promise.
- Scholarship: in *Economic Analysis of Law*, Posner treated an injunction as best suited to cases where damages are hard to measure, and noted that it can give the holder bargaining leverage beyond the value of the right.

**What would change the answer.**
1. The law governing the FRAND commitment, a US statute, or a Supreme Court ruling makes the steps binding.
2. The implementer refuses to post security or to be bound by a court-set rate, or a judgment against it cannot be collected. The case for an injunction is then strong, steps or not.
3. Reliable evidence that Huawei-style sequences shorten disputes and cut litigation costs without pushing rates above patent value, which would support adopting them as a safe harbor.
