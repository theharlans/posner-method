**Issue.** A party asks the Seventh Circuit to adopt the Huawei v. ZTE steps as the test for when the holder of a standard essential patent ("SEP") that made a Fair, Reasonable and Non-Discriminatory ("FRAND") commitment may get an injunction. The real question is whether a fixed negotiating sequence, built by the CJEU to police abuse of dominance under European Union ("EU") competition law, should be a gate for an American equitable remedy, or only evidence bearing on it.

One threshold point. Appeals in patent infringement cases go to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, so the question would reach the Seventh Circuit through a contract or antitrust claim about the FRAND commitment, for example a claim that seeking an injunction breached the undertaking. The analysis is the same either way.

The analysis starts from a premise. FRAND is a promise about the terms and conditions of a license, and the royalty is one of those terms. The European Telecommunications Standards Institute ("ETSI") policy speaks of FRAND "terms and conditions," and so do the other major policies. That matters here. The Huawei offer step asks for a written offer on FRAND terms that specifies, in particular, the royalty and how it is calculated, so the royalty is one required element of a broader offer.

**The economics.** A fight over an injunction is a fight over bargaining leverage. Rough numbers, all guesses: the implementer sells 20 million devices a year. The holder asks $2.00 per unit; the implementer says $0.50. The gap is $30 million a year. But the device earns the implementer $40 a unit, or $800 million a year, and the threat of exclusion lets the holder press for far more than $2.00. That excess is hold-up value, and it comes from the standard, not the invention. Without any injunction risk, the implementer can delay, pay nothing, and at worst pay $2.00 years later. That is holdout. Any rule chooses which of the two problems it tolerates.

*What the steps do well.* They force information into the open. The holder knows its portfolio, its claim charts and its comparable licenses, so it is the cheaper source of that information, and requiring it to notify and then make a specific written offer with an explanation puts the first move on the party who can make it most cheaply. The implementer knows its own sales and can post security cheaply if it really means to pay. Security is the key step. Once the disputed $30 million a year is secured, delay stops paying, and the main reason to enjoin an unwilling licensee mostly disappears. The steps sort hold-up from holdout using conduct a court can observe.

The preliminary injunction formula already rewards that conduct. Relief is proper only if P x Hp > (1 - P) x Hd, where P is the holder's chance of winning, Hp its irreparable harm without relief, and Hd the implementer's irreparable harm from a wrongly granted injunction. Posted security drives Hp toward zero. Halted sales and hold-up leverage make Hd large. No separate gate is needed to get the right answer.

*What the steps do badly.* First, a fixed sequence invites gaming of form. A holder can make a written, explained offer at an inflated rate. An implementer can make a prompt counteroffer at a token rate and post security on its own number. Every box is checked, and the court is back to deciding whose terms are FRAND, which the steps were supposed to spare it.

Second, a rate-centered offer step misses most of what FRAND covers. A holder can offer a defensible rate wrapped in terms no comparable licensee accepted: a worldwide portfolio license the implementer does not need, a broad grant-back, a confidentiality clause that hides comparables, or a one-sided forum clause. That offer passes a royalty-focused step and still fails FRAND on scope, reciprocity, or non-discrimination in process.

Third, a bright-line gate raises error costs. An injunction wrongly granted because an implementer missed a step pulls a product line and hands the holder hold-up value. An injunction wrongly denied because a holder's offer was imperfect costs the holder delay, which interest can cure. The first error is much costlier, so a gate that trips on procedural missteps is a poor fit.

*Institutional competence.* A court is good at testing a rate against comparable licenses and at judging whether a party bargained in good faith. It is poor at writing a whole license. The useful core of Huawei is that it leaves the non-price terms to the parties under a duty of good faith a court can police. The economics runs out on what "fair" means between firms with different business models. There the analysis rests on contract text and good faith, not price theory.

*Legal fit.* Huawei answers a different question. It says when seeking an injunction is an abuse under Article 102 TFEU. A US court asks whether equity should grant one under the ordinary equitable factors. The ETSI undertaking is a contract governed by French law (clause 12), which requires good faith in negotiating and performing contracts (article 1104 of the French Civil Code). The Huawei sequence is good evidence of what good-faith negotiation looks like. It is not a rule of decision for an American remedy.

**Pragmatic outcome.** Treat the steps as evidence, not as a test. Negotiating conduct bears on irreparable harm, the adequacy of damages, and the balance of hardships. A holder that made a specific written offer on all material terms, explained both the rate and the non-price terms by reference to comparables, and met a refusal to counter or give security, has a strong showing of holdout. An implementer that countered promptly on all terms, posted security, and agreed to be bound by a court-set license has a strong showing that damages suffice. Neither side forfeits equity for missing a box.

This keeps the information-forcing benefit, avoids the form-gaming, and pairs with a remedy that handles holdout without keeping products from consumers: a court-set royalty with prejudgment interest, enhanced for bad-faith delay. Holders bear the cost of delay. Implementers bear the cost of security and interest.

**Likely holding.** We decline to adopt the Huawei v. ZTE framework as the test for injunctive relief. That framework construes Article 102 TFEU, which does not govern here. Whether to enjoin infringement of a FRAND-committed patent is a question of equity. A FRAND commitment is strong evidence that money is adequate compensation, so an injunction is ordinarily unavailable. The parties' negotiating conduct, including whether the holder made a specific offer on all material terms and whether the implementer countered and offered security, is relevant but not dispositive. An injunction remains available against an implementer that refuses to accept, or to be bound by, FRAND terms, or against whom a money judgment cannot be enforced.

**The formalist objection.** In its best form: the ETSI undertaking is a French-law contract, and the legal order that governs it applies Huawei. A US court construing that contract should read its good-faith duty the way the governing law does. SEP licensing is global, and a different American standard for the same portfolio invites forum shopping and conflicting decrees, so comity favors following the CJEU. And a clear sequence is cheaper to administer than a case-by-case equitable inquiry.

The reply concedes part of this. Huawei interprets EU competition law, not the ETSI contract, so it is not an authoritative reading of the contract's good-faith term. It is persuasive evidence of what that term requires, and the evidence-based approach gives it exactly that weight. Uniformity would not follow from adoption, because a US court would still apply its own equitable test to US patents, and the steps leave open whether each offer and counteroffer is FRAND. The administrative savings are smaller than they look for the same reason. The concession: if French law were shown to make these steps the content of good faith under the ETSI undertaking, the contract would bind, and the court should apply it even where the economics favors a looser standard.

**From the opinions.**
- Apple, Inc. v. Motorola, Inc., 869 F. Supp. 2d 901 (N.D. Ill. 2012): a FRAND commitment concedes that a royalty is adequate compensation, which cuts against an injunction, and the royalty should exclude hold-up value.
- American Hospital Supply Corp. v. Hospital Products Ltd., 780 F.2d 589 (7th Cir. 1986): interim relief turns on P x Hp > (1 - P) x Hd, weighing a wrongly granted injunction against a wrongly denied one.
- Lake River Corp. v. Carborundum Co., 769 F.2d 1284 (7th Cir. 1985): separates what the economics suggests from what binding law requires; Huawei does not bind, so it is weighed on its merits.
- In *Economic Analysis of Law* Posner argued that an injunction can give the right holder bargaining leverage worth more than the right itself, and in *The Problems of Jurisprudence* he treated precedent as an important input to a forward-looking judgment, not a mechanical answer.

**What would change the answer.**
1. The standards development organization ("SDO") policy, or the parties' own agreement, adopts a negotiating sequence. It is then a contract term, and the court enforces it.
2. French law is shown to make the Huawei steps the content of good faith under the ETSI undertaking.
3. Evidence that implementers routinely evade court-set royalties or sit beyond enforcement, so damages cannot deter holdout. That would strengthen the case for a firmer gate.
